# Seminal Papers

I've put together this list to anchor the curriculum's historical narrative (see Module 0) in actual scientific literature, not just tutorials and blog posts. It's split into two chronological lists: general AI-milestone papers, and AI-hardware-specific milestone papers. Both lists have absorbed the papers that started as honorable mentions, once each was verified against a primary source.

Citation keys follow the `[@authorYYYY]` syntax defined in the curriculum's citation conventions, so any module can cite these entries directly in prose or in its own `## References` section.

## General AI-milestone papers

Papers, in chronological order, commonly considered the major turning points for artificial intelligence in the broad sense, not hardware-specific.

- **[@mcculloch1943]** McCulloch, W. S. and Pitts, W. (1943). *A Logical Calculus of the Ideas Immanent in Nervous Activity*. First mathematical model of an artificial neuron, and the conceptual basis from which all neural computing starts. https://doi.org/10.1007/BF02478259
- **[@turing1950]** Turing, A. M. (1950). *Computing Machinery and Intelligence*. Poses "can a machine think?" and proposes the Turing Test, foundational for AI as a discipline before the term itself existed. https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/LIX.236.433
- **[@rosenblatt1958]** Rosenblatt, F. (1958). *The Perceptron*. First trainable neural network model with a learning algorithm. It generated enormous enthusiasm, then the stop imposed by Minsky and Papert (1969), who demonstrated its limits (the XOR problem): the direct cause of the first "AI winter". https://doi.org/10.1037/h0042519
- **[@rumelhart1986]** Rumelhart, D. E., Hinton, G. E. and Williams, R. J. (1986). *Learning Representations by Back-Propagating Errors*. Makes multi-layer network training practical: the algorithm essentially all of modern deep learning rests on. https://doi.org/10.1038/323533a0
- **[@hochreiter1997]** Hochreiter, S. and Schmidhuber, J. (1997). *Long Short-Term Memory*. Solves the vanishing-gradient problem in sequences; for almost twenty years the standard for NLP, speech recognition, and time series. https://doi.org/10.1162/neco.1997.9.8.1735
- **[@lecun1998]** LeCun, Y. et al. (1998). *Gradient-Based Learning Applied to Document Recognition*. LeNet-5, CNNs applied to real problems, and the basis of modern computer vision. https://doi.org/10.1109/5.726791
- **[@krizhevsky2012]** Krizhevsky, A., Sutskever, I. and Hinton, G. E. (2012). *ImageNet Classification with Deep Convolutional Neural Networks* (AlexNet). The watershed moment: it demonstrates that deep learning plus GPUs plus big data clearly beats every prior approach on ImageNet. The deep learning boom starts from here. https://doi.org/10.1145/3065386
- **[@goodfellow2014]** Goodfellow, I. et al. (2014). *Generative Adversarial Networks*. Introduces the adversarial paradigm for generation, the direct progenitor of most image and video generative AI before diffusion models. https://doi.org/10.1145/3422622
- **[@vaswani2017]** Vaswani, A. et al. (2017). *Attention Is All You Need*. The Transformer. Probably the most influential paper of the last decade: the architecture behind BERT, GPT, and substantially every modern LLM. https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.03762
- **[@silver2016]** Silver, D. et al. (2016). *Mastering the Game of Go with Deep Neural Networks and Tree Search* (AlphaGo). Deep RL plus Monte Carlo Tree Search beats human champions at a game considered "impossible" for machines, a milestone for reinforcement learning. https://doi.org/10.1038/nature16961
- **[@devlin2018]** Devlin, J., Chang, M. W., Lee, K. and Toutanova, K. (2018). *BERT: Pre-training of Deep Bidirectional Transformers for Language Understanding*. Introduces deep bidirectional pretraining, becoming the NLP fine-tuning standard until GPT-style few-shot approaches took over. https://arxiv.org/abs/1810.04805
- **[@brown2020]** Brown, T. B. et al. (2020). *Language Models are Few-Shot Learners* (GPT-3). Demonstrates large-scale few-shot in-context learning without fine-tuning, the direct precursor to the ChatGPT-era API business model. https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.14165
- **[@ho2020]** Ho, J., Jain, A. and Abbeel, P. (2020). *Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models*. The foundational paper behind modern diffusion-based image and video generation, alongside GANs above. https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.11239
- **[@ouyang2022]** Ouyang, L. et al. (2022). *Training Language Models to Follow Instructions with Human Feedback* (InstructGPT). Introduces the RLHF alignment technique that turned raw GPT-3 into a usable assistant, the direct technical precursor to ChatGPT. https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.02155
- **[@jumper2021]** Jumper, J. et al. (2021). *Highly Accurate Protein Structure Prediction with AlphaFold*. The highest-profile "AI applied to science" result, solving the 50-year-old protein folding problem. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-021-03819-2

## AI-hardware-specific milestone papers

Milestones, in chronological order, following specifically the hardware and compute-architecture thread: the hardware that made practical AI implementation possible, as opposed to purely algorithmic milestones above. AI progress has been driven as much by algorithms as it has been enabled, or blocked, by available hardware.

- **[@vonneumann1945]** Von Neumann, J. (1945). *First Draft of a Report on the EDVAC*. The stored-program architecture underlying almost every modern computer. Without this, there is no substrate to run anything on.
- **[@rosenblatt1958hw]** Rosenblatt, F. (1958). *Mark I Perceptron*. Not just the algorithm, but the physical machine built at Cornell Aeronautical Laboratory: synaptic weights implemented with motorized potentiometers. The first hardware dedicated to a neural network.
- **[@kung1979]** Kung, H. T. and Leiserson, C. E. (1979). *Systolic Arrays for VLSI*. A dataflow compute architecture designed for massively parallel matrix operations. It sounds like a niche detail, but it's explicitly the architectural lineage Google's TPU descends from, as Google says themselves in the 2017 paper below. This is the direct architectural lineage behind the systolic-array artifact targeted by curriculum Module 2.
- **[@mead1990]** Mead, C. (1990). *Neuromorphic Electronic Systems*. Carver Mead coins the term "neuromorphic computing" and shows how to implement neural functions in analogue VLSI. A different lineage from the digital/GPU thread, but foundational for all neuromorphic hardware research since (today's Intel Loihi, IBM TrueNorth, and so on). https://doi.org/10.1109/5.58356
- **[@lindholm2008]** Lindholm, E. et al. (2008). *NVIDIA Tesla: A Unified Graphics and Computing Architecture*. The IEEE Micro paper describing NVIDIA's G80/CUDA architecture, the moment the GPU stops being purely graphics hardware and becomes a massively parallel general-purpose processor. The technical hinge that makes everything else below possible. https://doi.org/10.1109/MM.2008.31
- **[@raina2009]** Raina, R., Madhavan, A. and Ng, A. Y. (2009). *Large-Scale Deep Unsupervised Learning Using Graphics Processors*. First clear demonstration that GPUs accelerate deep learning by an order of magnitude (10-70x) over CPUs. A direct precursor to AlexNet, often under-cited but crucial. https://doi.org/10.1145/1553374.1553486
- **[@krizhevsky2012]** Krizhevsky, A., Sutskever, I. and Hinton, G. E. (2012). *AlexNet*. Already listed above, but here specifically for its hardware angle: trained on two consumer-grade GTX 580s, not datacentre hardware, demonstrating that GPU plus deep learning beats everything else on ImageNet. The paper that convinces industry to invest in GPUs for AI at scale.
- **[@dean2012]** Dean, J. et al. (2012). *Large Scale Distributed Deep Networks*. Google's DistBelief paper: how to distribute training across thousands of CPU machines. A direct precursor to TensorFlow and to all the distributed-training infrastructure indispensable for large models today.
- **[@jouppi2017]** Jouppi, N. P. et al. (2017). *In-Datacenter Performance Analysis of a Tensor Processing Unit*. The TPU v1 paper: the first custom ASIC designed specifically for machine learning (inference, later training), based on a systolic-array architecture, building directly on the Kung and Leiserson lineage (entry 3 above). Marks the start of the dedicated-AI-chip era (TPU v2-v6 and various custom NPUs and accelerators follow). https://doi.org/10.1145/3079856.3080246
- **[@hooker2021]** Hooker, S. (2021). *The Hardware Lottery*. Closes the loop: argues that a research idea's success in AI often depends more on how well it fits the hardware available at the time than on its intrinsic quality. Explicitly retraces AI-hardware history (perceptron, backprop, CNN, LSTM) through this lens. Useful as an interpretive frame for the whole list above. https://doi.org/10.1145/3467017
- **[@micikevicius2017]** Micikevicius, P. et al. (2017). *Mixed Precision Training*. Shows FP16 training on Tensor Cores roughly halves memory and compute cost with no accuracy loss, the software-side unlock that made Tensor Core hardware (already in the Lindholm/Jouppi lineage above) actually usable at scale. https://arxiv.org/abs/1710.03740
- **[@kaplan2020]** Kaplan, J. et al. (2020). *Scaling Laws for Neural Language Models*. Not hardware itself, but the paper that economically justified pouring ever more hardware investment into ever-larger models, directly shaping the compute-buildout wave that follows. https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.08361

Honorable mention: more recent work on wafer-scale chips (Cerebras) and domain-specific LLM-inference chips, left out because no single paper anchors it.
