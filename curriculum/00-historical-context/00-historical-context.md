# Module 0: Historical Context

Before I touch a single accelerator topic, I want to walk through how we got here. This module isn't nostalgia. It's the reasoning behind why AI hardware today looks the way it does, and why some "obviously correct" hardware bets failed while others, built on the same underlying idea, succeeded a decade later. I'll come back to that pattern more than once as we move through the eras below.

I'm using three primary dimensions to measure hardware design across this history: Power, Performance, and Area. These three aren't independent choices: they're the core trade-offs of digital electronics, the "golden triangle" that every hardware designer navigates. Raising performance or shrinking area almost always costs more power. Lowering power almost always means accepting slower performance or using more chip area. This triangle isn't a single named theorem with one founder, but it emerged as the industry standard through decades of engineering practice. The closest academic foundation is Clark D. Thompson's formalization of the area-time trade-off for VLSI circuits in the late 1970s [^thompson1979], which proved mathematical limits on simultaneous improvements in speed and area. For decades, this trade-off was softened by Dennard scaling, the pattern IBM researcher Robert Dennard described in 1974 [^dennard1974]: as transistor size shrank, voltage and current scaled proportionally, keeping power density constant. In other words, designers could improve performance and shrink area together while the power cost stayed hidden. That stopped working around 2005-2007, once leakage current and threshold voltage stopped scaling with transistor size, and power joined area and performance as an explicit, irreducible constraint [^esmaeilzadeh2011]. From that era onward, every choice appears in the PPA triangle. I'll use this lens across the eight eras below.

## Era 0: Computational Origins (pre-1950)

I'm starting this narrative on ENIAC (Electronic Numerical Integrator and Computer), not on Turing's 1936 computability work. That's a deliberate choice, so let me explain it before going further.

Turing's 1936 paper defines what's computable at all, via the Universal Turing Machine, and it's genuinely foundational [^turing1936]. But it has no hardware artifact. It's a theory-of-computation result, not a machine you can point to. ENIAC, on the other hand, is a specific machine, dedicated on a specific date, built from specific components. I can immediately measure it through the PPA framework: that's the point of this module.

ENIAC was unveiled on 14 February 1946 at the University of Pennsylvania, under a US Army wartime contract. It was the first large-scale, general-purpose, programmable electronic digital computer, built from around 18,000 vacuum tubes rather than mechanical relays, which is what let it run at genuinely electronic speed instead of being bottlenecked by moving parts. Multiple independent institutional sources describe it as the start of the electronic computer age, not just "a milestone" [^ethw1946] [^ieeespectrum2026] [^penntoday2021].

Plotted as the first historical point on the PPA graph, ENIAC dominates the area and power axes for all the wrong reasons: it sits at the extreme end of both, while its performance per watt is negligible. The machine occupied roughly 170 square meters and drew 150 kilowatts, yet performed around 5,000 additions per second, roughly 500 floating-point operations per second. That tradeoff, enormous footprint and power for tiny computational throughput, is the defining point: ENIAC proved electronic computation was possible, but the architecture itself was brutally inefficient. The stored-program concept from EDVAC (Electronic Discrete Variable Automatic Computer), ENIAC's successor, showed how to escape this trap: by making machines programmable without rewiring, the next generation could reuse the same physical footprint and power budget to run different algorithms, improving the area-to-performance and power-to-performance ratios.

There's a sequencing quirk worth flagging here. Von Neumann's "First Draft of a Report on the EDVAC" was distributed in June 1945, roughly eight months before ENIAC's public dedication, and it contains the first published description of the stored-program architecture that bears his name [^vonneumann1945]. That looks backwards at first: the architecture concept predates the machine that inspired it. It isn't really backwards, though. Von Neumann had joined the ENIAC team by around September 1944, after learning of the project that August, so his report captures the conceptual leap that came out of working on ENIAC, published before ENIAC itself was unveiled to the public. I'm treating 1945-46 as one compressed origin beat rather than forcing strict date order onto it: ENIAC proved a general-purpose electronic machine could be built at all, and the EDVAC report described how to make the next one programmable without rewiring it by hand.

One more entry belongs in this era, and it predates both of the above. McCulloch and Pitts published "A Logical Calculus of the Ideas Immanent in Nervous Activity" in 1943, the first mathematical model of an artificial neuron [^mcculloch1943]. Their move was to formalize neuron firing as propositional logic: given a handful of physical assumptions, a neuron fires or doesn't (all-or-none, no in-between), a neuron fires when enough of its synapses are excited within a fixed time window regardless of what happened before, the only meaningful delay is the synapse itself, treated as one discrete time step, and a single active inhibitory synapse blocks firing outright. Under those assumptions, whether a neuron fires at a given time step can be written as a logical proposition, and its dependence on the neurons feeding into it becomes a logical implication built from AND, OR and NOT, exactly which one falls out of how its excitatory and inhibitory synapses are wired and how high its firing threshold is. That's the paper's real result: excitatory and inhibitory wiring doesn't just resemble boolean logic, it directly instantiates it, so any expression in propositional logic can in principle be built out of a net of these idealised neurons. It has no hardware behind it at all, it's pure logic and mathematics, but it's the conceptual seed that every neural-network hardware story in this curriculum eventually traces back to. It's worth sitting with the fact that this idea existed three years before ENIAC was switched on.

The last beat of this era is a component-level story rather than a machine-level one. Bardeen and Brattain first amplified a signal with a point-contact semiconductor device on 16 December 1947 at Bell Labs, with the public announcement following on 30 June 1948 [^computerhistory1947] [^ieeeusa2022]. The transistor drew little contemporary attention at the time, which is easy to miss in hindsight given it's the physical substrate that eventually replaces the vacuum tube entirely, and underwrites every Moore's-Law-era scaling story that follows it. I'm placing it here, right after ENIAC, because that's the order these things actually happened: general-purpose electronic computation existed first, running on vacuum tubes, and only afterwards did the component that would eventually replace them get invented.

## Era 1: Symbolic Founding, First Hype (1950-73)

Turing published "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" in 1950. He sidesteps the question "can machines think?" as too loaded to answer directly, and replaces it with the Imitation Game: can a machine's conversation be mistaken for a human's [^turing1950]. It's a philosophical paper, not a hardware one, but it's the first time the question of machine intelligence gets asked in a form precise enough to be tested, and the Turing Test still shapes how "AI progress" gets framed in the press today. Six years later, the field would get a name and a research identity of its own.

The Dartmouth Conference, held over the summer of 1956, is where "artificial intelligence" gets named as a distinct field, organised by McCarthy, Minsky, Rochester, and Shannon [^dartmouth1956]. Before this, what we're calling AI was just part of "computing" generally. After it, AI has a name, a research identity, and eventually its own funding cycles, which matters for the eras that follow this one.

Two years after Dartmouth, in 1958, Rosenblatt published the Perceptron, the first trainable neural network model with an actual learning algorithm [^rosenblatt1958]. What I want to draw attention to here is that this wasn't just an algorithm on paper. The same year, the Mark I Perceptron was built as a physical machine at Cornell Aeronautical Laboratory: a 20x20 grid of photocells acted as its "retina," feeding 400 inputs into a layer of randomly-wired association units, whose weights were stored and adjusted using motorised potentiometers. Trained on simple geometric shapes, it could learn to tell them apart after a handful of examples [^rosenblatt1958hw]. That's worth pausing on: the first hardware built specifically for a neural network is fifteen years older than most people would guess, and it's analog, electromechanical, and nothing like the digital accelerators the rest of this curriculum focuses on. It's still the direct ancestor of the whole lineage.

The Mark I Perceptron is also where this module's recurring hype pattern first shows up. On 8 July 1958, the New York Times ran "NEW NAVY DEVICE LEARNS BY DOING", after a Navy-organised press conference, speculating that the machine would eventually "walk, talk, see, write, reproduce itself and be conscious of its existence" [^nytimes1958]. This is the first documented AI hype and expectation-gap cycle I'll be pointing back to later in this module, when the same shape of overpromise-then-funding-cut repeats in Era 2 and again in Era 3. It's worth being honest that the coverage itself is well documented, but the direct causal link between this specific press cycle and the AI winter that eventually followed is more of an interpretive claim than a settled fact.

Not every strand of the field's early years ran through hardware, though. The dominant paradigm through the 1950s and 60s was symbolic AI, often called GOFAI (Good Old-Fashioned AI): intelligence built from hand-coded rules and logical inference over symbols, rather than learned from data. Newell, Shaw and Simon's General Problem Solver, reported in 1957 and published in 1959, is the concrete anchor for this: a single program meant to solve any problem expressible as a set of formal rules and a goal state, using means-ends analysis to close the gap between the two [^newell1959]. Learning-based approaches weren't absent, though. That same year, Arthur Samuel coined the term "machine learning" itself, in a paper describing a checkers-playing program that improved by playing against itself [^samuel1959]. The two approaches, rule-based and learning-based, coexisted from the field's earliest years; it's only in hindsight that one looks like the default and the other the exception.

Joseph Weizenbaum built ELIZA in 1966, the first chatbot, using simple pattern matching and scripted substitution to simulate a Rogerian psychotherapist [^weizenbaum1966]. The name isn't an acronym: it's a nod to Eliza Doolittle from George Bernard Shaw's *Pygmalion*, a character trained to sound refined despite her humble origins, mirroring how the program turned rigid, mechanical input into natural-sounding conversation. There's no understanding behind it at all, just string matching against a script, but Weizenbaum was unsettled by how readily his own secretary and other users attributed real understanding and empathy to it, some asking to be left alone with the program. That reaction is what later got named the "Eliza effect": the tendency to read genuine comprehension into a system that's mechanically simple, once it produces the right surface behaviour. It's a pattern that resurfaces every time a new generation of language systems gets mistaken for something more than it is.

There's a more direct, technical cause for the funding collapse described in Era 2, and it belongs here rather than there. Minsky and Papert published *Perceptrons* in 1969, a rigorous mathematical treatment of what the Perceptron architecture could and couldn't compute, and it showed that a single-layer Perceptron can't solve XOR, a genuinely basic case of a not-linearly-separable problem [^minskypapert1969]. That's not a funding story or a press cycle, it's a proof, and it lands squarely on the hardware Rosenblatt had built and championed. The NYT hype cycle above set unrealistic public expectations; this book gave researchers and funders a rigorous technical reason to stop believing the specific architecture could deliver on them. Both strands feed into Era 2.

## Era 2: First AI Winter (1973-79)

AI's first winter was triggered by two nearly simultaneous policy shocks in 1973. Sir James Lighthill, the Lucasian Professor of Mathematics at Cambridge, delivered a devastating 49-page report to the British Science Research Council concluding that AI had massively overpromised and spectacularly underdelivered [^lighthill1973]. The "combinatorial explosion" problem, the exponential growth in search space that no algorithm could overcome, was insurmountable with the techniques then available. Within months, the UK government gutted AI funding at all but two universities [^lighthill1973].

Across the Atlantic, the US Congress passed the Mansfield Amendment in the same year, barring ARPA from funding research without direct military application [^mansfield1973]. DARPA's response unfolded over the next several years: the agency let its five-year Speech Understanding Research program, which had committed roughly $3 million annually across CMU and four other labs (SRI, MIT Lincoln Laboratory, SDC, and BBN), run its course, then declined to fund the planned five-year follow-on, ending the program around 1976 [^nationalacademies1999]. The irony is that DARPA had funded this research with explicit promises of speech recognition systems that could understand unrestricted, continuous speech, and CMU's Harpy and Hearsay systems had arguably met those original benchmarks, but internal disputes over how the tests were run left many at DARPA unconvinced the program had delivered scalable, general solutions [^nationalacademies1999].

The technology that triggered the crackdown was symbolic AI: expert systems like Dendral (Stanford, 1965) [^lindsay1993] and MYCIN (Stanford, 1972) [^shortliffe1975] that encoded domain-specific knowledge as logical rules. These systems worked brilliantly in narrow domains but couldn't learn from data, couldn't generalize to novel problems, and required expensive manual knowledge engineering from human experts. They dominated the area and power axes of the PPA triangle, MYCIN alone ran on a single time-shared PDP-10 mainframe accessed over ARPANET, and building out its ~600 rules for each new domain still took years of manual knowledge engineering, and delivered tiny performance gains in return. The field had promised artificial general intelligence; what it delivered was expensive, brittle, domain-specific tooling. By the late 1970s, funding had reportedly dried up across both the UK and the US, though this narrative is itself contested.

Mainstream sources including Holloway's "Making Things Think" describe a clear 1974-1980 funding collapse as a definitive first AI winter [^giacaglia2022]. A dissenting ACM/CACM opinion piece goes further, arguing that the "first AI winter" of the 1970s never happened at all, that the AI research community grew steadily through that decade, and that the real funding bust came later, in the late 1980s, following the collapse of the expert-system boom [^haigh2023]. The debate remains unresolved. No fixed milestone paper anchors this era, it is a documented gap in the academic literature, not an oversight.

## Era 3: State Bets & Second AI Winter (1979-97)

Even as the first AI winter gripped the US and UK, specialized AI hardware was being designed and deployed, and would fail catastrophically. This era represents the era's most expensive hardware gamble and its most instructive failure within the PPA framework. Its dates deliberately overlap Era 2's by two years, 1979-80: the work below was already underway while the winter described in Era 2 was still nominally in effect, and I'd rather show that concurrency honestly than force a clean handoff onto two things that were genuinely happening at the same time.

Two architectural innovations emerged from academic research in the late 1970s and 1980s. H.T. Kung and Charles Leiserson formalized the concept of systolic arrays in 1979 [^kung1979] (presented in 1979, published the following year in Mead and Conway's *Introduction to VLSI Systems*) homogeneous networks of processing elements arranged in a regular geometric pattern, each performing a simple computation and passing results to neighboring elements in a rhythmic, clock-synchronized flow. The name drew an analogy to the rhythmic pumping of the heart. The systolic architecture promised to reduce the power and area overhead of data movement in VLSI by keeping computation local and rhythmic. Carver Mead pioneered a different path: neuromorphic electronic systems, formalizing in 1990 the approach of mapping biological neural-information processing directly onto analog VLSI circuits [^mead1990]. Mead's insight was that for many ill-conditioned input problems, biological solutions were many orders of magnitude more efficient than digital methods, because biological systems used elementary physical phenomena as computational primitives rather than simulating them in software. Both approaches aimed to escape the combinatorial explosion of symbolic AI by building hardware that more closely matched the computational structure of the problem domain.

The fundamental theoretical breakthrough, backpropagation, arrived in the same period. Rumelhart, Hinton, and Williams published their reinvention of the backpropagation algorithm in 1986, proving that multi-layer neural networks could be trained efficiently [^rumelhart1986]. This was the missing piece: a learning algorithm that worked. But for a decade, nobody deployed it, because the hardware available was slow, memory-constrained, and utterly mismatched to the algorithm's needs.

Meanwhile, Japan bet decisively on specialized hardware. In 1982, Japan's Ministry of International Trade and Industry (MITI) launched the Fifth Generation Computer Systems (FGCS) Project: a ten-year, ~¥57 billion (~$320 million USD) program aimed at building massively parallel computers optimized for logic programming and AI inference. The bet was explicit: general-purpose commodity computers would hit fundamental limits, and custom VLSI designed specifically for AI workloads would dominate the market. The Japanese government established the Institute for New Generation Computer Technology (ICOT) to execute the vision. By the time the project concluded in 1992, it had spent its full budget and delivered no commercially deployable systems. Applications remained in demonstration form only [^ishikawa1993]. The problem was architectural mismatch: the FGCS systems were optimized for inference on fixed symbolic knowledge bases. The world was about to need learning systems, not reasoning systems, and the hardware couldn't pivot.

The specialized Lisp machine market collapsed at the same moment. Symbolics, Lisp Machines Inc., and other vendors had built entire workstations optimized for symbolic AI and expert systems. In 1986, Symbolics had $115 million in revenue. By 1987, a $10,000-$30,000 commodity workstation from Sun or Digital Equipment Corporation could run Lisp and expert-system software competitively with a $100,000+ Symbolics Lisp machine. The market vanished overnight. Lisp Machines Inc. went bankrupt in 1987; Symbolics filed for Chapter 11 protection in January 1993 [^symbolics1993]. The specialized hardware optimized for yesterday's AI paradigm was worth exactly what a general-purpose commodity machine cost.

This era is the defining cautionary contrast to Era 5. Here, specialized hardware lost to commodity machines because the AI paradigm shifted and the hardware couldn't follow. In Era 5, specialized hardware (Google's TPU) will win precisely because the AI paradigm, deep learning on massive matrices, plays to the systolic-array architecture that Kung and Leiserson imagined. This is the Hardware Lottery in action [^hooker2021]: the same architectural idea can be a dead-end in one era and a triumphal success in another, depending on which software trends happen to match the hardware's strengths. Success depends less on the quality of the hardware idea and more on lucky alignment with the algorithms that happen to dominate the time the hardware arrives in the market.

## Era 4: Preconditions for Deep Learning (1997-2012)

One paradox defines this era: symbolic AI achieved its most stunning public victory just as the field was abandoning it.

On 11 May 1997, Deep Blue, a specialized chess engine built by IBM, defeated Garry Kasparov, the world's reigning chess champion, in a six-game match [^deepblue1997]. The final score was 3.5 to 2.5, a narrow margin that masked how comprehensively the machine had outplayed the human in the games that mattered. The event captured something unprecedented: a machine had beaten humanity's best at something considered the ultimate test of strategic intelligence.

Deep Blue won not through learning or neural networks, but through brute-force search and hand-coded evaluation functions: pure symbolic AI, exactly the paradigm that had failed to deliver in the 1970s and 80s. That it succeeded so dramatically disguises the actual story. Deep Blue was a $10 million, single-purpose machine, optimized down to custom VLSI for a single game. Its area and power consumption were enormous relative to what a general-purpose computer of equivalent era could achieve, and its "intelligence" was wholly domain-specific. It was the most expensive, most specialized, and most narrow proof that symbolic AI could still dominate within its chosen box. Era 3 had seen specialized hardware fail; Deep Blue proved that specialized hardware built for the right task, at the right moment, could succeed spectacularly. Yet the field was already moving away from the game-playing, symbolic reasoning paradigm that Deep Blue embodied.

During this same decade, machine learning was quietly moving from research laboratories into everyday consumer infrastructure, years before the deep learning revolution would make this shift explicit. In the late 1990s and early 2000s, researchers began deploying Naive Bayes classifiers to filter spam email, with Paul Graham's 2002 "A Plan for Spam" becoming the best-known early demonstration [^graham2002]. The approach was startlingly simple: treat spam and non-spam as two categories, count word frequencies in each, and use conditional probability to classify incoming messages. It worked well enough that by the early 2000s, Naive Bayes-based spam filters were becoming standard in enterprise mail systems. Over at Amazon, engineers had patented an item-to-item collaborative filtering system in 1998 and deployed it in the early 2000s, quietly revolutionising how e-commerce recommendations worked [^linden2003]. What's significant is the pattern: machine learning was already shifting from research to infrastructure, handling billions of decisions daily, years before neural networks would dominate the headlines.

The theoretical foundation for deep learning was already being laid, though few outside academia noticed. Yann LeCun's LeNet, a convolutional neural network trained to recognise handwritten digits, showed that neural networks could be trained efficiently on practical problems [^lecun1998]. Sepp Hochreiter and Jürgen Schmidhuber published their work on Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks in 1997, solving a critical problem that had plagued recurrent neural networks for years: the vanishing-gradient problem that made it nearly impossible to train networks on long sequences [^hochreiter1997]. Both papers were technical, addressed to specialists, and largely overlooked by the broader AI community, which was still betting on symbolic approaches. Yet they were collecting the pieces: Hinton, LeCun, and Bengio sustained a research effort through the 2000s to revive neural networks as a serious computational paradigm, work that the ACM would later recognise with the 2018 A.M. Turing Award [^turingaward2018].

But there was a hard constraint nobody could escape: the hardware wasn't ready.

As the introduction describes, Dennard scaling broke down around 2005-2007, ending three decades of free performance gains. The consequences were immediate: clock speeds hit a thermal wall, single-threaded performance scaling was over, and the industry had to pivot. Multi-core systems, specialized silicon, and heterogeneous compute became mandatory, not optional. This physics-level constraint would prove critical to what came next.

In response, two key infrastructure pieces came together. NVIDIA released CUDA in 2006, a framework that let researchers write general-purpose code for graphics processors [^nvidia2006]. The Tesla line of GPUs followed in 2007, specifically marketed for parallel compute rather than just graphics [^nvidia2007]. These weren't the first GPUs, but they were the first to make it genuinely easy to run arbitrary algorithms on massive parallelism. Around the same time, in 2006, Amazon launched EC2, its Elastic Compute Cloud, letting anyone rent computing power by the hour over the internet [^amazon2006]. None of these developments felt revolutionary at the time. CUDA was a curiosity. EC2 was an experiment. But they were laying the material conditions for the next era: abundant parallel compute, instantly accessible, waiting for someone to use it. The PPA trade-off had fundamentally shifted: power per unit area was now a constraint, which meant that generalist hardware like CPUs was forced to compete on specialisation. GPUs, with their thousands of cores optimized for simple repeated operations, sat perfectly at that new trade-off point.

The final precondition arrived in 2009. Fei-Fei Li and colleagues released ImageNet, a dataset of 14 million images hand-annotated with object labels, together with an annual competition challenging researchers to classify images into a thousand categories [^deng2009]. It was enormous. The dataset itself would require rethinking how machine learning systems were evaluated and trained. Three years later, this dataset would become the stage on which deep learning's vindication would play out.

Before the deep learning explosion, one more milestone deserves mention. On 14 February 2011, IBM's Watson defeated the world's strongest Jeopardy! champions in a televised match [^watson2011]. Jeopardy! required understanding natural language questions, retrieving relevant facts from vast databases, and confidence-scoring candidate answers. Watson was built on symbolic AI principles: hand-coded rules, statistical ranking, and expert systems. It won decisively. Yet this victory, like Deep Blue's, was a dead-end for the broader trajectory of AI. It proved that symbolic systems could dominate narrow, well-defined domains; it didn't suggest a path toward general AI. Within a year, the field's energy had completely shifted to deep learning on GPUs.

## Era 5: Deep Learning + GPU Era (2012-17)

On 30 September 2012, the results of the ImageNet Large Scale Visual Recognition Challenge were published, and the field's entire trajectory changed. A team from the University of Toronto, led by Alex Krizhevsky, submitted a convolutional neural network called AlexNet that achieved a top-5 error rate of 15.3 per cent, vastly outperforming the second-place entry at 26.2 per cent [^krizhevsky2012]. It was a decisive victory. What mattered wasn't just the margin. It was that Krizhevsky had trained this deep network on GPU clusters using NVIDIA's CUDA framework. The convolutional network was decades old, inherited from LeCun's LeNet. But the training method, the scale of data, and the compute substrate had all aligned for the first time. Deep learning went from a curiosity to an empirical necessity in a single competition cycle.

That same year, Google's researchers published DistBelief, a framework for training deep neural networks across multiple computers and GPUs [^dean2012]. What DistBelief made possible was previously unthinkable: using hundreds or thousands of machines simultaneously to train a single model on datasets too large for any single computer to hold. The implications cascaded. Suddenly, the bottleneck wasn't algorithmic insight, it was compute availability. If you had enough GPUs, enough data, and enough patience, you could throw raw parallel compute at almost any problem in computer vision or natural language processing and beat human performance. This was radically different from the symbolic AI era: you didn't need experts to hand-code rules. You needed data and compute.

Meanwhile, OpenAI was founded in December 2015 as a non-profit by Sam Altman, Elon Musk, and others, with the explicit mission to develop artificial general intelligence safely [^openai2015].

The era's most iconic moments came when deep neural networks solved problems experts believed were still decades away. In November 2016, Google released its Neural Machine Translation system, an end-to-end deep learning approach to translating between languages [^wu2016]. Overnight, translation quality jumped. Users noticed. News outlets covered it. For the first time, deep learning's capability was obvious not to academics watching benchmark scores, but to ordinary people seeing tangibly better results from a consumer service.

In March of that same year, AlphaGo defeated Lee Sedol, one of the world's strongest Go players, in a four-game match, winning 4 to 1 [^silver2016]. Go had been considered the last frontier of AI, a game of intuition and long-term strategy that machines were thought to be decades away from mastering. AlphaGo combined deep reinforcement learning (trained by playing millions of games against itself) with tree search, and it worked. Game 2 produced Move 37, a move that no human professional would have played, and that Lee Sedol took as a moment to pause and compose himself, recognising he was facing something alien. It became the emblematic moment: a machine's pattern-recognition and strategic thinking visibly diverging from human intuition, not through brute force, but through something that looked like genuine understanding.

Yet the most important thread tying this era together isn't the benchmarks or the victories. It's the alignment of the Hardware Lottery. In Era 3, specialized hardware optimized for symbolic AI and logic programming had failed catastrophically. The FGCS systems and Lisp machines had been engineered to perfection for inference over fixed knowledge bases. Then the world needed learning systems, and the hardware couldn't pivot. General-purpose commodity computers, awkward as they were, could be programmed to do anything. In Era 5, specialized hardware returns. In 2017, Norbert Jouppi and colleagues at Google published details of the Tensor Processing Unit (TPU), a custom-built accelerator optimized specifically for the matrix multiplications at the heart of deep neural networks [^jouppi2017]. The TPU succeeded where specialized AI hardware had failed before, not because it was better engineered, but because the software paradigm had evolved to match its strengths. Deep learning, at scale, is primarily matrix algebra. Systolic arrays, which Kung and Leiserson imagined back in 1979, are perfectly suited for repeating matrix operations. The world needed learning systems, and specialized hardware designed for matrix workloads could now deliver them at unprecedented scale. That's the Hardware Lottery in its clearest form: the same architectural idea can be worthless in one era and transformative in another, depending purely on which algorithms happen to dominate when the hardware arrives.

One final paper anchors this era's technical foundation. In 2017, Vaswani and colleagues published "Attention Is All You Need", describing the Transformer architecture [^vaswani2017]. The Transformer abandoned recurrence entirely, replacing it with a pure attention mechanism that could process all positions in a sequence in parallel. It was simple, elegant, and immediately better than the recurrent-network approaches that had dominated natural language processing. More importantly, the Transformer's parallelism meant it scaled perfectly with GPUs and TPUs. It was hardware-aware in the deepest sense: the algorithm and the substrate had co-evolved. By the end of 2017, Transformers were already being adopted across the field. Within five years, they would subsume nearly every major architecture in deep learning.

## Era 6: Scale, Access, Mass Awareness (2017-22)

This era is defined by a simple inversion: what had been research, locked behind APIs and research institutions, became infrastructure. The Transformer had solved the architectural problem. The scale had begun. What remained was to see what would happen when you gave ordinary people access to it.

For its first three years, OpenAI operated in research mode, publishing papers and conducting experiments. Then came GPT-1 in June 2018, a 117-million-parameter language model trained on BooksCorpus, a dataset of roughly 7,000 unpublished books, which showed that large-scale unsupervised language modelling could learn useful representations [^radford2018]. It was a proof of concept. The paper was technical, addressed to researchers. Nobody outside academic circles noticed.

GPT-2 arrived in February 2019, with 1.5 billion parameters and an order of magnitude more training data [^radford2019]. This time, OpenAI staged its release carefully. Rather than publishing weights immediately, the organisation released the model in stages, citing concerns about misuse. The reasoning was explicit: a language model of this scale could generate convincing synthetic text, and the lab didn't want bad actors deploying it for spam or disinformation before safeguards existed. This staged approach was novel in AI. It suggested that the field was maturing: people were thinking about deployment consequences, not just capability.

The next step arrived on 28 May 2020, when OpenAI published the GPT-3 paper [^brown2020]. The model was enormous: 175 billion parameters, trained on 570 gigabytes of text from the internet, books, and code. More importantly, GPT-3 demonstrated in-context learning: give it a few examples of a task, and it could perform that task without retraining. The paper showed GPT-3 doing translation, arithmetic, question-answering, and creative writing, all from the same underlying model. No fine-tuning. Just pure scaling and clever prompting. The world took notice. By June 2020, OpenAI had released a limited API access to GPT-3, beginning the transition from research artifact to commercial service.

GPT-3 marked a threshold moment in hardware and software alignment. Training a 175-billion-parameter model requires staggering amounts of compute: specialized TPU clusters, optimized floating-point arithmetic, and careful distributed-training orchestration. The inference side is equally demanding. But the Transformer's parallelism, designed a decade earlier with no thought to LLMs, maps perfectly onto GPUs and TPUs. The algorithm and substrate had co-evolved. When GPT-3 arrived, the hardware was ready. More importantly, the infrastructure was ready: cloud providers offered on-demand GPUs by the hour, and the economic model of "pay for compute as you go" made it feasible for researchers to experiment without building their own data centres.

Meanwhile, the generative model story was expanding beyond text. In January 2021, OpenAI released DALL-E, a model that generated images from text descriptions [^ramesh2021]. DALL-E showed that the scaling hypothesis, throw more data and compute at a well-designed architecture, and capability emerges, applied to vision and language jointly, not just to text alone. Google's Imagen, released in 2022, pushed the boundary further, achieving photorealistic image generation [^saharia2022]. But the most significant contrast came with Stable Diffusion, released by Stability AI in August 2022 [^rombach2022]. Stable Diffusion used the same core ideas as Imagen: diffusion models, which iteratively remove noise from random images guided by text conditioning. But Stability released the model weights publicly, made it runnable on consumer-grade GPUs, and licensed it permissively. Imagen remained locked behind Google's closed APIs. DALL-E required OpenAI's subscription. Stable Diffusion ran on any machine with 8GB of VRAM. The open-source approach spread. Within months, researchers and artists were running Stable Diffusion locally, fine-tuning it, building on it, in ways that proprietary systems made technically and legally difficult. This split (open-weights, consumer-capable systems versus closed, cluster-only access) would define the competitive landscape from this point onward.

## Era 7: Present Frontier (2022-now)

The final beat of Era 6 was sudden. On 30 November 2022, OpenAI released ChatGPT to the public without an API key requirement: just sign up, log in, and chat. The response was unprecedented. ChatGPT reached 1 million users in five days, 100 million in two months, making it the fastest-growing consumer application in internet history [^openai2022]. What ChatGPT did wasn't new: it was a Transformer model fine-tuned with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF), a technique published years earlier, applied to conversation. But the quality, the ease of access, and the moment in the technology cycle aligned perfectly. Ordinary people, not researchers, could suddenly see what a large language model could do. The AI field's boundary shifted from "research discipline" to "consumer infrastructure" overnight.

The six months following ChatGPT's release saw the most concentrated competitive scramble in the history of artificial intelligence. Google, despite having invented the Transformer and published Imagen, was reportedly caught off guard. According to internal reports, the company declared a "code red" in December 2022, signalling the urgency with which executives viewed the competitive threat [^grant2022]. Within twelve months of ChatGPT's launch, Microsoft had integrated GPT models into its Office suite and search engine, Amazon had invested up to $4 billion in Anthropic, the AI safety-focused company Dario Amodei and his sister Daniela co-founded in 2021 after leaving OpenAI [^anthropic2021], Meta had released its LLaMA models to researchers despite internal debate over the risks of open access, and Elon Musk had launched xAI in July 2023 with explicit ambitions to compete directly [^reuters2023].
 
The landscape fractured along lines established earlier in the era. OpenAI maintained a closed-weights, API-first model: GPT-4 was more capable than GPT-3, but you couldn't download it or run it locally. Meta and Stability AI pushed open-weights alternatives: models you could inspect, fine-tune, and deploy yourself. Anthropic, backed by Amazon, competed in the same market but kept its Claude models closed-weights, accessible only via API. Neither approach dominated completely. Enterprises preferred the closed models, with their stability guarantees and OpenAI's support. Researchers and open-source communities preferred the alternative, with its transparency and freedom to modify. The competitive dynamic was no longer "AI labs versus the world"; it was "closed platforms versus open ecosystems."
 
One architectural development marked the frontier: test-time compute as a scaling axis. For years, progress in language models came from two sources: scale of training (bigger models, more data) and compute at training time (more GPUs, longer training runs). In September 2024, OpenAI released a preview of o1, which introduced reasoning as an explicit, separate scaling axis [^openai2024a]. o1 doesn't just generate the next token; it generates a chain-of-thought internally, working through a problem step-by-step before answering, spending more computational effort on harder problems. December 2024 brought the full release [^openai2024b]. The o1 approach suggested that raw token generation wasn't the ceiling for capability; instead, problem-solving depth, how much internal computation a model invests before answering, was an independent and powerful lever. This split the scaling story in two: training-time compute and inference-time compute, each with its own returns curve.
 
The open-weights side of the field found its own answer to test-time compute a few months later, from an unexpected source. In January 2025, the Chinese company DeepSeek released R1, a reasoning model matching o1 on several benchmarks, together with its full weights and technical report under a permissive open license [^deepseek2025]. R1 built on DeepSeek's V3 base model, released the previous month, which the company's own technical report priced at roughly $5.6 million in GPU compute, a fraction of what frontier labs were assumed to spend [^deepseek2024]. The release triggered a broader reassessment of the open-weights ecosystem: R1 was the first frontier-class open model to come out of China rather than the US, and it was followed by other Chinese labs, including Alibaba's Qwen and Moonshot AI's Kimi, releasing competitive open-weight reasoning models of their own. Whether or not the training-cost figures held up to scrutiny, the episode reinforced the pattern already visible in the West: open-weights releases were no longer a niche alternative but a credible way to compete at the frontier.
 
The most striking development of this era, however, was not a straightforward increase in training scale, but a redistribution of where that scale was spent. Pre-training compute growth for flagship closed-weights models actually slowed by 2025. OpenAI's GPT-5, released in August 2025, was estimated by independent analysts to have been trained on less raw pre-training compute than its own predecessor, GPT-4.5, breaking OpenAI's historical pattern of roughly one-hundred-fold compute growth per generation [^epochai2025]. Anthropic's flagship model, Claude Opus 4.5, released three months later in November 2025, told a similar story: rather than a dramatic leap in pre-training scale over its 2024-2025 predecessors, its gains came largely from refinements downstream of pre-training [^anthropic2025]. What grew explosively instead was post-training and reinforcement-learning compute, extending the pattern set by o1: labs found that scaling the reasoning stage delivered better returns, faster, than scaling raw pre-training, so growth shifted from one axis to the other rather than compounding on both simultaneously. Some analysts estimated this post-training compute was scaling roughly ten-fold every four months in 2025, a pace that, if sustained, would soon rival total pre-training budgets outright [^epochai2026]. The open-weights community, while unable to match the pre-training budgets of the largest labs, kept pace on this front, as reasoning techniques and RL recipes spread quickly through published research and open releases such as DeepSeek's. This combination of a plateauing pre-training scale and an exploding post-training compute defined the training landscape through 2025 and into the present.
 
As of today, August 2026, the boundary of the field has shifted again, and further than the 2024-2025 developments above might suggest. Large language models are no longer a research question; they are infrastructure. The PPA trade-off, which has defined this entire module, remains central, but its terms have moved once more: the accelerators themselves are increasingly commoditised, the core algorithms are broadly understood, and the binding constraint has shifted to power. Frontier labs are now racing to build gigawatt-scale data center campuses: OpenAI's Stargate program, announced in January 2025, targets $500 billion in investment and 10 gigawatts of AI infrastructure in the US [^openai2025], while Meta's Prometheus and Hyperion campuses are designed to reach 1 and up to 5 gigawatts of capacity respectively [^meta2025]. A recent multi-expert international assessment concluded that, while chip manufacturing and data availability are likely to keep pace with demand through the end of the decade, electricity supply and grid interconnection have become the primary near-term bottleneck to further scaling, even as power buildouts on the scale of several gigawatts remain technically feasible [^aisafety2026].
